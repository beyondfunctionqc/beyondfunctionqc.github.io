01 / Call for Papers

The Self and the Modern Condition

The French existentialist Gabriel Marcel once diagnosed the defining problem of modernity as the “misplacement of the idea of function.” In his famous essay “On the Ontological Mystery,” he describes modern society as essentially empty because it increasingly understands both the world and the person in terms of utility, efficiency, productivity, quantifiable performance, and similar measures. In such a climate, the human person is easily reduced to a collection of functions rather than recognized as a unique and irreducible self. Marcel’s critical analysis of the modern condition—as well as those of early existentialists such as Martin Buber, Karl Jaspers, Martin Heidegger, and Søren Kierkegaard—raised serious concerns and broader philosophical questions that continue to confront us today, nearly a century later. How do contemporary social, technological, political, and economic structures shape our self-understanding? Do they promote authentic selfhood, or do they encourage forms of alienation that obscure what it means to be human? Do they encourage objectified relationships that reduce others to I–It relations? Do they promote forms of self-objectification that contribute to mental health issues?

This conference invites students and scholars to explore questions bearing on the relationship between the self and the modern world. We seek philosophical reflection on the nature of selfhood and the ways in which contemporary realities influence our understanding of personal identity, agency, freedom, community, and what it means to live “the good life.” We are particularly interested in papers that examine how modern technological, scientific, philosophical, social, political, or religious developments contribute to—or challenge—our understanding of the self and the conditions of a good, meaningful, and fulfilling human life. Deeper philosophical reflection on the self enables us to reconsider or re-envision not only who we are, but also the kinds of conditions necessary for genuine human flourishing. We welcome papers that creatively engage these questions from historical or contemporary perspectives, or that draw upon interdisciplinary approaches to illuminate the nature of the self in the modern world.

02 / Suggested Topics

Suggested Topics

Preference will be given to papers related to the following topics:

  1. What does it mean to be a self in the modern world?
  2. Can religion provide practical or ontological grounding amid the perils of modern existence, or is modernity antithetical to religious and spiritual life?
  3. What does it mean to flourish, and how should we reconsider the good life under modern conditions?
  4. Can we still become ourselves? How should we understand authenticity, alienation, and meaning?
  5. Are we truly autonomous? What does agency mean for the modern self?
  6. How has technology—including social media, automobiles, airplanes, firearms, and artificial intelligence—transformed selfhood?
  7. How does artificial intelligence influence human self-understanding?
  8. Who are we online, and how does social media shape the digital self?
  9. When does a person become a product? How does consumer culture commodify the self?
  10. What would it mean for artificial intelligence to be a self, and how should we relate to it?
  11. What moral issues are most pressing as artificial intelligence continues to develop?
  12. How does globalization affect culture and personal identity?
  13. What can existentialism and phenomenology teach us about selfhood?
  14. What have classical, modern, and contemporary philosophical approaches taught us about the self?

Thinkers of interest

We are especially interested in—though not limited to—papers engaging thinkers such as Søren Kierkegaard, Gabriel Marcel, Karl Jaspers, Emmanuel Levinas, Martin Buber, Simone Weil, Charles Taylor, Alasdair MacIntyre, Hannah Arendt, Paul Ricoeur, Edith Stein, Maurice Merleau-Ponty, and other philosophers whose work contributes to our understanding of selfhood, personhood, and the challenges of modern existence.

03 / Organizer

About the Organizer

Anthony Malagon is a lecturer at Queens College. He has a Ph.D. in philosophy from Purdue University. He teaches courses in philosophy of religion, ethics, existentialism, and Eastern philosophy. He takes both existential and analytic approaches to questions of knowledge, truth, religion, ethics, and the self. He is editor of the collection The Religious Existentialists and the Redemption of Feeling and has a forthcoming book, An Existential Proof of God.
